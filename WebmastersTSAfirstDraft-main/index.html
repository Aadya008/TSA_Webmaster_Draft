MODULE 01

AI Foundations: The Mathematical Engine Room

+250 XP Available

How machines transition from hand-written deterministic logic to statistical parameter optimization across continuous multidimensional manifolds.

Lesson 1.1: Classical Logic vs. Empirical Optimization

Traditional computing uses deterministic logic: software engineers manually author if/else statements for all known cases. But complex real-world challenges—like understanding natural language or diagnosing medical scans—have far too many combinations for manual coding. Machine Learning (ML) inverts this by feeding inputs ($X$) and ground-truth answers ($Y$) into algorithms that optimize weights ($W$) and bias terms ($b$):

ŷ = σ(W • x + b)

During training, weights update via calculus gradient descent. During inference, parameters freeze so models compute predictions on new inputs through fast matrix multiplications.

Lesson 1.2: Transformers & Scaled Dot-Product Attention

Older recurrent networks (RNNs) processed text sequentially ($t_1 \to t_2 \to t_3$). By token 80, early signals faded due to vanishing gradients. The Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017) reads all sequence tokens in parallel by mapping inputs into Query ($Q$), Key ($K$), and Value ($V$) matrices:

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax((Q • K^T) / √d_k) • V

The dot product computes compatibility scores between all word pairs simultaneously. The scaling factor keeps numbers balanced in high-dimensional spaces so the softmax calculation maintains healthy gradients.

Lesson 1.3: Vector Embeddings & Loss Minimization

Models convert tokens into coordinate vectors in 512-to-4096-dimensional space. Semantic closeness is calculated with Cosine Similarity. To train the network, Categorical Cross-Entropy Loss evaluates the gap between the predicted word distribution and the actual target:

Loss = -Σ y_i • log(ŷ_i)

Backpropagation applies the calculus Chain Rule backwards through all layers to nudge weights in the direction of minimal error.

Lesson 2.1: The C.R.A.F.T. Prompt Framework

Vague, conversational questions lead to shallow answers. To calibrate an AI for academic rigor, use the C.R.A.F.T. protocol:

  • C - Context: Define the background, curriculum constraints, and source boundaries.
  • R - Role: Assign a specialized persona (e.g., "Act as an AP Biology evaluator").
  • A - Action: Command explicit analytical verbs (e.g., "Synthesize," "Isolate logical flaws").
  • F - Format: Request structured outputs (e.g., a 3-column Markdown table, a 4-step proof).
  • T - Target Audience: Calibrate the reading level, technical depth, and tone.
FAILING PROMPT:

"Explain cell division for my homework."

Result: Shallow, uncontrolled scope prone to oversimplification.
C.R.A.F.T. CALIBRATED PROMPT:

"Context: AP Biology cell cycle. Role: College biochemistry instructor. Action: Compare anaphase in mitosis vs. anaphase I in meiosis. Format: 3-column table on chromosome counts, spindle attachments, and genetic diversity. Target Audience: 10th-grade honors scholar."

Result: Structured, calibrated, and academically rigorous.
Lesson 2.2: Stochastic Hallucination Triage

Large Language Models are probabilistic next-token predictors. Given an input sequence, the model samples words according to learned probabilities. Because its objective function rewards statistical plausibility rather than empirical truth, models frequently assemble realistic-sounding yet entirely fabricated research papers, journal titles, and DOIs. Never cite an LLM directly as an authoritative secondary source—always cross-check DOIs on Google Scholar, PubMed, or JSTOR.

Lesson 2.3: Socratic Dialogue & Code Tracing

Instead of having AI write assignments, use it as a sparring partner to critique drafted arguments: "Act as an academic reviewer. Challenge my thesis on the Industrial Revolution with three counterarguments." When coding in Java or Python, supply your written script and ask the model to step through variable states line-by-line to isolate logical bugs.

The Ethical Compass: Algorithmic Bias, Privacy, & Honor Codes

+250 XP Available

Investigate dataset skew, civil rights disparate impact metrics, federal privacy laws, and academic honor codes.

Lesson 3.1: Algorithmic Bias & The Four-Fifths (80%) Rule

Models do not possess personal malice; they reflect statistical distributions present in historical training datasets. If historical datasets underrepresent a demographic cohort, backpropagation dedicates less parameter capacity to that group, causing prediction confidence to drop and error rates to rise. Under federal regulatory compliance (EEOC Uniform Guidelines), an automated selection process produces adverse disparate impact if the selection rate for a protected demographic is less than 80% (4/5) of the selection rate for the highest group:

Selection Ratio = Rate(Protected) / Rate(Benchmark) < 0.80

If Group A is accepted at 80%, any selection rate for Group B below 64% ($0.80 \times 0.80$) flags disparate impact under federal standards.

Lesson 3.2: Student Privacy & FERPA Compliance

Commercial consumer AI tools routinely log prompt submissions to fine-tune future models. Submitting unredacted student IDs, disciplinary records, or medical accommodations into commercial AI interfaces violates federal privacy safeguards under the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA). Scholars must scrub all personal identifying information (PII) before submitting queries.

Lesson 3.3: The Academic Spectrum of Integrity
  • Zone 1: Socratic Study Partner (Ethical Scaffolding): Quizzing, concept review, and brainstorming analogies. Permissible; 100% student authorship.
  • Zone 2: Structural Outlining (Permissible with Disclosure): Critiquing outlines and discovering counterarguments. Permissible with teacher consent and attribution.
  • Zone 3: Automated Paraphrasing (Compromised): Running text through AI rewriters to evade detection. Academic dishonesty.
  • Zone 4: Direct Plagiarism (Honor Violation): Submitting AI-generated prose or fabricated citations as personal work. Strict honor code violation.
